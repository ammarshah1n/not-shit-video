# The craft that mattered

The accepted reference was a story-led film, not an effect reel. One fictional person's day: a short phone exchange, one stable desktop that changes within its frame, a real piece of work, and a thought captured away from the desk. The long cut gave text room to breathe; the short cut used fewer words rather than racing them.

## Direction

- Decide what the viewer should understand or feel before selecting an effect. One transformation, one proof action, one memorable outcome.
- Sell what the product does for its user, not private implementation detail. A private demonstration and public marketing have different disclosure boundaries.
- Real UI with fixtures beats an invented interface. For unavailable channels, a drawn representation is acceptable ONLY when labelled and faithful to the actual text/interaction contract.
- Claims need source evidence. Separate shipped, source-only, staged and imagined features. Never let an attractive storyboard turn an aspiration into a product claim.
- Use one fictional persona as the source for every name, date, message and screenshot. Invent names and use reserved domains such as example.com. Never paste personal account exports into fixtures.

## House direction, not a generic template

Near-black world, one accent hue, glass desktop, restrained display type, mono-cap chapter markers. Chapters belong on the negative-space band above the product. Plain English; no visible em-dash-heavy copy or generic hype. You can change the palette for a real brand; keep the hierarchy and restraint.

The window's geometry stays continuous while its content dissolves. Camera motion is slow and motivated. Show activity inside the product: a typed query, a progress state, a meaningful result. Do not replace proof with a drifting screenshot.

A phone emerges from darkness before its frame fully appears. Incoming text waits in a typing state, arrives, then HOLDS. A voice-note waveform and receipt are events with explicit frame times, not CSS loops. Sounds are original synthesis, not ripped device notifications.

## Reading is the pacing constraint

- Main proof text: about 4–5 words per second plus a beat.
- Captions / lower thirds: about 3 words per second plus one second.
- Start the allowance when words appear, not at the start of a transition.
- Streaming text needs both a readable reveal and a completed-answer hold.
- Keep critical words legible in the 1080p/phone-sized delivery copy, not just the 4K editor.
- If the 30-second cut cannot hold the text, remove a beat or shorten the text. Do not accelerate all content.

In the successful revision, replies that held for roughly 4–5 seconds grew to roughly 10–11 seconds; long reveal/answer passages reached roughly 18–19 seconds. The full film grew from about 75 seconds to about 119 seconds. That was a deliberate improvement, not a failure to edit.

## One hierarchy

Never stack a large headline AND a caption making the same claim. For tilted screens, reserve the top headline band and pin the screen's transform origin beneath it; rotation/scale must grow away from the type. A gradient caption scrim covering product pixels hides the very evidence the scene needs.

Type that types itself is useful inside product interaction. Brand cards may simply hold or use a restrained focus pull. The earlier grammar sheet's 'type everything' line is not stronger than the later accepted film or legibility.

## Sound and the close

One musical arc: quiet start, build under the busiest proof, resolve, then silence. Narration was roughly 30% of the accepted long cut, not wall-to-wall explanation. Duck music under each line. Stop the score before the end card. Leave a real silent hold after the closing words.

## Approve the premise cheaply

Render three representative stills and a short motion excerpt before the full film. A pristine 3D graph flight was once rejected despite successful technical tests. 'Smooth', 'zero hard cuts' and 'looks expensive' are not substitutes for a useful story. See production-lessons.md.
